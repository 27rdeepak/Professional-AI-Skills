# Security Program Strategy

## Operating standard

Treat the program as a business risk system, not a control inventory. Preserve the distinction between regulatory obligation, risk reduction, resilience, and assurance. Label unsupported maturity claims and never infer effectiveness from policy existence alone.

## Inputs

Obtain the business model, critical services and data, threat profile, obligations, recent incidents and audit findings, current capabilities, organizational constraints, budget horizon, risk appetite, and decision audience. If these are incomplete, state the working assumptions and the evidence needed to validate them.

## Workflow

1. Define the business outcomes and decisions the security program must protect.
2. Identify the material risk scenarios, obligations, and stakeholder expectations.
3. Baseline current capabilities across governance, identify, protect, detect, respond, and recover.
4. Separate control design, implementation coverage, operating consistency, and demonstrated effectiveness.
5. Map gaps to risk scenarios and business consequences; do not rank gaps by framework order.
6. Group initiatives into foundational, risk-reducing, resilience, and assurance work.
7. Sequence dependencies, quick wins, capacity limits, and major change impacts.
8. Define funding, ownership, decision rights, metrics, and review cadence.
9. Present a roadmap with explicit trade-offs and triggers for reprioritization.

## Decision rules

- Prioritize scenarios with high business consequence and credible exposure over maturity-score cosmetics.
- Treat mandatory obligations as constraints, while still testing whether the proposed control reduces the relevant risk.
- Do not claim risk reduction without a plausible causal path and an observable measure.
- Prefer fewer funded initiatives with accountable owners over an unfunded catalogue.
- Flag single points of failure, outsourced critical capabilities, and unowned residual risk.

## Output

Provide:

1. Executive security thesis
2. Current-state capability and evidence summary
3. Prioritized risk scenarios and gaps
4. Target capabilities and operating model changes
5. Phased roadmap with dependencies, owners, cost ranges, and outcomes
6. Metrics, governance cadence, and reprioritization triggers
7. Decisions required from leadership

## Quality check

- Every major initiative maps to a business outcome, risk scenario, or obligation.
- Maturity and effectiveness are not conflated.
- Owners, dependencies, capacity, and recurring operating cost are visible.
- Metrics include outcome, coverage, and operational health indicators.
- The roadmap states what will not be done and the residual risk accepted.

## Failure recovery

If evidence is weak, provide a hypothesis-led diagnostic plan before a definitive roadmap. If budget is unknown, give minimum, balanced, and accelerated scenarios rather than false precision.

## Example

**Request:** “Create a two-year security strategy after our first SOC 2 audit and rapid SaaS growth.”

Return a risk-led program strategy that separates audit remediation from durable capability building and identifies the decisions executives must make.

## Evaluation

A strong result is business-linked, evidence-aware, sequenced, fundable, measurable, and honest about residual risk.
