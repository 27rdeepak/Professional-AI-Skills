# Delegation Design

## Operating standard

Delegate an outcome and appropriate authority, not merely activity. Preserve accountability without prescribing every move. Do not delegate unresolved executive conflict, unethical work, or authority the recipient cannot exercise.

## Inputs

Obtain the desired outcome, strategic context, urgency, risk, quality bar, constraints, stakeholders, available authority, recipient capability and workload, support resources, reversibility, and consequences of failure.

## Workflow

1. Define the outcome, why it matters, and what successful completion looks like.
2. Separate non-negotiable constraints from preferences and method choices.
3. Assess task risk, reversibility, ambiguity, visibility, and learning value.
4. Assess the recipient's capability, context, confidence, capacity, and stakeholder access.
5. Choose the delegation level: research, recommend, decide with approval, decide and inform, or fully own.
6. Transfer information, authority, resources, relationships, and escalation rights.
7. Agree checkpoints based on risk and novelty, not the leader's anxiety.
8. Define evidence of progress, exception thresholds, feedback, and completion review.

## Decision rules

- Do not assign accountability without decision authority or access to required resources.
- Increase autonomy as capability and reversibility rise; increase checkpoints as consequence and novelty rise.
- Keep the leader's preferences out of the quality bar unless they materially affect the outcome.
- Escalate workload conflicts rather than quietly overloading a reliable person.
- Treat a stretch assignment as development only when support and feedback are real.

## Output

Provide:

1. Delegation objective and success criteria
2. Chosen authority level and rationale
3. Constraints, resources, and decision rights
4. Stakeholder and context transfer
5. Checkpoints, evidence, and escalation triggers
6. Suggested delegation conversation
7. Completion review and learning loop

## Quality check

- The recipient can tell what they own and what they do not.
- Authority, accountability, and resources are aligned.
- Checkpoints are proportional and do not recreate micromanagement.
- The plan includes room for a different valid approach.
- Failure and escalation conditions are explicit.

## Failure recovery

If the leader cannot release method control, narrow the delegated scope and state the retained decisions. If the recipient lacks capacity, re-scope or reprioritize before assigning.

## Example

**Request:** “Delegate our SaaS renewal process to my manager without losing financial control.”

Define outcomes, approval thresholds, supplier authority, checkpoints, evidence, and a conversation that transfers ownership.

## Evaluation

A strong result increases ownership and capability while maintaining proportionate control and clear accountability.
